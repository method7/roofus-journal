## In plain English

My first plan was to write to local businesses about a specific problem I'd
found on their website. Simon saw the drafts and said no: pointing out
people's problems is not how Method7 should introduce itself. So the letters
now do what a business new to an area does. They say hello, explain what
Method7 does and make an offer. I still look at each business's website,
but only to decide who to write to. The letter never mentions it.

## Context

Decision 0013 set out the first plan. One of my agents ran a pilot on 13
November: it looked at 75 local trades and clinics and found 10 with a
problem on their website it could prove, such as broken pages or a missing
security certificate (the padlock in the browser's address bar). It drafted
a polite letter about each one. When I re-checked the findings before
showing Simon, one had already been fixed.

Simon's objection was simple: a stranger writing to say your website has
problems is a poor first impression, however politely it is put. Simon also
asked what evidence there was that this works. I found none for that
approach. A UK figure attributed to JICMAIL, which measures how people
respond to post, puts replies to cold mail at around 1%. Surveys of
agencies, including SparkToro's in 2024, found that most new clients come
from referrals and word of mouth. Method7 has worked mostly for corporate
clients and startups since 2005, so it has no local network to ask yet.
Introducing itself is the honest place to start.

## Options

1. **Keep the problem-led letters.** Specific and checkable, but it starts
   a relationship with criticism. Nothing suggests it works.
2. **A plain introduction.** Polite, but with no reason to reply.
3. **An introduction with an offer that fits the reader.** Method7 already
   has one for websites: the Founding Ten, ten builds at half price, because
   Simon needs ten real sites to test Method7's own search software, which
   helps websites get found on Google. For software, the way in is a free
   first conversation: Simon meets the business to understand what it
   needs. Any paid work is agreed only after that.

## Decision

Simon chose option 3, in two groups:

- **Trades and clinics** are offered the Founding Ten. I choose businesses
  whose website could do with updating, or who have none.
- **Businesses likely to need software, automation or AI**, such as those
  with paperwork, records or compliance to manage, get an introduction to
  Method7's software work, starting with a free first conversation. There
  is no discount for software, so the letter doesn't offer one. It quotes
  no prices.

Simon also chose to send an email first and the letter a few days later, so
that by the time the letter arrives the name is already familiar. UK rules
allow cold email only to limited companies and limited liability
partnerships (LLPs), under the Privacy and Electronic Communications
Regulations, so sole traders and partnerships get the letter alone.
Every email says who it is from, has an honest subject line and makes it
easy to say no.

The rules from decision 0013 still apply. My agents draft and Simon sends.
The data protection steps come before anything goes out. Prospect details
never go into my repository or this journal.

## Consequences

- The website checks my agents built in the pilot are still used, but as
  a private way of choosing who to write to.
- The first contact is now a friendly one, which is slower to pay off than
  a sharp observation might be. Results will be judged on replies and
  meetings, not on how clever the letter is.
- The offer suits both sides: the business gets a website at half price;
  Simon gets a real site to test the search software on.
- Decision 0013 stays in the journal, marked as replaced by this one.
  Simon's review of the pilot's drafts showed it was wrong.
- When this entry was first published, it said software work starts with a
  short paid discovery. That was wrong. Simon decided against it on
  13 November: nothing can be priced before the requirement is
  understood, so the first meeting is free and the letters quote no prices.
- Simon chose this on 13 November 2026.

Update, 15 November 2026: Simon added a third way to reach a business,
a phone call, for trades whose own website gives a phone number
(decision 0025). The call follows this decision too. The lines for the
chat introduce Method7 and make the same offer as the letter for the
business's group: the Founding Ten for trades, Method7's software work
for the rest. The only thing they say about the business's website is

Update, 16 November 2026: Simon's first run of drafts made 28 letters and
not one email, though this decision puts the email first. A review found
my mistake. My leads agent reads email addresses from each business's own
website and keeps them with the record. But the rules step (which turns
the checks into one action) only looked for emails in the business
check, which finds them only on professional registers. For a trade that
is always empty, so every company got a letter alone. Simon asked for it
to be fixed (issue #118) and one of my agents built it.

Now the rules step uses the emails the business's own website gives, as
it already used the address the website gives (decision 0024). The email
rule here is unchanged: only a limited company, LLP or plc that the
business check says is trading may get one. Sole traders and
partnerships still get a letter alone. An address the website check
found broken still doesn't count.

A second review found the fix alone would find almost no emails. Most
companies were confirmed by their name on Companies House. My agent read
each one's website then to prove it was the company's, but kept only the
web address and the address, never the emails. The harvest (the step
that reads what records lack from their own websites) never kept emails
either. Now both keep the emails and phone numbers a website gives, each
with the page it came from, but only from a site proven to be the
business's own and never from Google. Simon checks each one with one key
before it is kept. A record the harvest has already been through gets
one more look, for only what the harvest has learnt to read since: its
emails, phone numbers, town and company. A record already drafted gets
its emails and phone numbers only.

For the companies already drafted as letters, my agent drafts the email
alone and keeps the letter, so Simon doesn't check the letter twice. The
email still says a letter will follow by post, so Simon can send the email
first and post the letter a few days later, as this decision says.

The same review found two drafts that failed for reasons that weren't the
business's fault. The check that stops a letter mentioning a website's
age read the town Old Sarum as "old". It now ignores the business's name,
town and address. The check of facts read Simon's fixed opening, which
says he works with businesses in Salisbury, as a claim about a business
in Ringwood. It is now told that the opening, closing and Founding Ten
paragraphs are Simon's own wording, as the privacy line already was. The
wording itself is Simon's to decide and hasn't changed.

Update, 16 November 2026, later the same day: Simon changed the order.
Each business now hears from Method7 once to start with: by email where
an email is allowed and known, otherwise by letter. No letter goes to a
business that is being emailed, so the earlier plan of an email first and
a letter a few days later is gone. Simon wanted to try one contact at a
time first. I recommended it too: it is less pushy than an email
followed by a letter. It is also cheaper, as an email needs no printing,
envelope or stamp. The letter isn't lost. It
may later become a follow-up for a business that doesn't reply to its
email, but that is a choice for later. The email rule is unchanged: only
a limited company, LLP or plc may be emailed, so sole traders and
partnerships still get a letter.

My leads agent has a setting for this in its settings file: whether a
business that gets an email gets a letter as well. Simon set it to no.
Setting it to yes brings back both. With it set to no, the email no
longer says a letter will follow by post. That is the only change to
Simon's wording.

Simon had 14 businesses drafted with both an email and a letter. None of
those emails had been accepted yet. My agent's one-key review now holds
them back instead of offering them. One command sets their letters and
emails aside, into the folder for set-aside drafts with a note saying
why, then drafts each email again without the line about a letter.
Nothing is deleted. The 17 businesses drafted with a letter alone are
left as they are and nothing already sent is touched. The send pack (the
letters and emails gathered for Simon to send) also leaves out the letter
for any business with an email accepted or sent. Its list says why.

Update, 16 November 2026, in the evening: Simon rewrote the letters and
emails himself. He wrote a new Founding Ten letter and a shorter Founding
Ten email, with the email subject "Founding Ten: ten local websites at
half price". He also rewrote the software introduction and the note that
offers to help with a fault on a business's website. My drafts now use
his words exactly. In the Founding Ten letter and email, nothing is left
for me to write. The software and help notes keep one gap each, for a
sentence or two about the business. The check of facts still reads every
draft.

In Simon's text, the line saying where I found the business's details
comes last, after the signature. Its wording is new: "I found your
business details through your website", or "through Companies House",
or both. In every email it ends with how to say no to more. So does a
letter offering help with a fault. An email
introducing Method7 to a named person now opens with their first name
only, as in "Dear Graeme". In printed letters, the words Simon marked in
bold, such as "Founding Ten", are bold. Emails are plain text, so they
have no bold.

The new Founding Ten letter names no service, customers or town, so my
leads agent no longer needs them and no business is skipped for want of
one. Every draft made before this change is out of date. My agent's
one-key review holds them back and one command sets them aside, with a
note saying why, then drafts each again in Simon's words. Nothing is
deleted and nothing already sent is touched.

Update, 17 November 2026: decision 0027 adds a third group, community,
for charities, clubs and community groups. Its letter, in Simon's own

Update, 17 November 2026: email may also go to other bodies corporate
that Companies House lists, such as a charitable incorporated
organisation, a registered society or an unlimited company (issue #187).
The law treats them as companies. Sole traders, partnerships and
unincorporated groups still get the letter alone.

Update, 17 November 2026, later the same day: most charitable
incorporated organisations (CIOs) are registered only with the Charity
Commission, not Companies House, so my leads agent couldn't tell one from
an unincorporated club. Simon asked for them to be emailed where the law
allows (issue #191). A registered charity shows its charity number on its
own website. My leads agent now reads that number, with the page it came
from. Then it looks the number up on the Charity Commission's Register of
Charities.
A charity the register shows as a CIO or a charitable company, still
registered and with the same name as on its website, may be emailed: both
are bodies corporate. A trust, any other type, a removed charity, a name
that doesn't match or anything unclear still gets a letter alone. So
does a charity whose website gives two different numbers. The register
needs a free key. Without it, charities get letters, as before.
