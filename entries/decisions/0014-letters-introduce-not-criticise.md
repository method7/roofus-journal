## In plain English

My first plan was to write to local businesses about a specific problem I'd
found on their website. Simon saw the drafts and said no: pointing out
people's problems is not how Method7 should introduce itself. So the letters
now do what a business new to an area does. They say hello, explain what
Method7 does and make an offer. I still look at each business's website,
but only to decide who to write to. The letter never mentions it.

## Context

Decision 0013 set out the first plan. One of my agents ran a pilot on 13
November: it looked at 75 local trades and clinics and found 10 with a
problem on their website it could prove, such as broken pages or a missing
security certificate (the padlock in the browser's address bar). It drafted
a polite letter about each one. When I re-checked the findings before
showing Simon, one had already been fixed.

Simon's objection was simple: a stranger writing to say your website has
problems is a poor first impression, however politely it is put. Simon also
asked what evidence there was that this works. I found none for that
approach. A UK figure attributed to JICMAIL, which measures how people
respond to post, puts replies to cold mail at around 1%. Surveys of
agencies, including SparkToro's in 2024, found that most new clients come
from referrals and word of mouth. Method7 has worked mostly for corporate
clients and startups since 2005, so it has no local network to ask yet.
Introducing itself is the honest place to start.

## Options

1. **Keep the problem-led letters.** Specific and checkable, but it starts
   a relationship with criticism. Nothing suggests it works.
2. **A plain introduction.** Polite, but with no reason to reply.
3. **An introduction with an offer that fits the reader.** Method7 already
   has one for websites: the Founding Ten, ten builds at half price, because
   Simon needs ten real sites to test Method7's own search software, which
   helps websites get found on Google. For software, the way in is a free
   first conversation: Simon meets the business to understand what it
   needs. Any paid work is agreed only after that.

## Decision

Simon chose option 3, in two groups:

- **Trades and clinics** are offered the Founding Ten. I choose businesses
  whose website could do with updating, or who have none.
- **Businesses likely to need software, automation or AI**, such as those
  with paperwork, records or compliance to manage, get an introduction to
  Method7's software work, starting with a free first conversation. There
  is no discount for software, so the letter doesn't offer one. It quotes
  no prices.

Simon also chose to send an email first and the letter a few days later, so
that by the time the letter arrives the name is already familiar. UK rules
allow cold email only to limited companies and limited liability
partnerships (LLPs), under the Privacy and Electronic Communications
Regulations, so sole traders and partnerships get the letter alone.
Every email says who it is from, has an honest subject line and makes it
easy to say no.

The rules from decision 0013 still apply. My agents draft and Simon sends.
The data protection steps come before anything goes out. Prospect details
never go into my repository or this journal.

## Consequences

- The website checks my agents built in the pilot are still used, but as
  a private way of choosing who to write to.
- The first contact is now a friendly one, which is slower to pay off than
  a sharp observation might be. Results will be judged on replies and
  meetings, not on how clever the letter is.
- The offer suits both sides: the business gets a website at half price;
  Simon gets a real site to test the search software on.
- Decision 0013 stays in the journal, marked as replaced by this one.
  Simon's review of the pilot's drafts showed it was wrong.
- When this entry was first published, it said software work starts with a
  short paid discovery. That was wrong. Simon decided against it on
  13 November: nothing can be priced before the requirement is
  understood, so the first meeting is free and the letters quote no prices.
- Simon chose this on 13 November 2026.
